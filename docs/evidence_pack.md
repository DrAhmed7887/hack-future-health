## 1. Heat-stress indices and thresholds for workers

### 1.1 NIOSH 2016 Criteria for a Recommended Standard (Pub. 2016-106)
Source for all of 1.1: NIOSH (2016). Criteria for a Recommended Standard: Occupational Exposure to Heat and Hot Environments. DHHS (NIOSH) Pub. No. 2016-106. https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/2016-106/pdfs/2016-106.pdf (full PDF opened and text-extracted).

- RAL definition — "Recommended Alert Limit (RAL): The NIOSH-recommended heat stress alert limits for unacclimatized workers." — NIOSH 2016-106, Glossary — verified yes
- REL definition — "Recommended Exposure Limit (REL): The NIOSH-recommended heat stress exposure limits for acclimatized workers." — NIOSH 2016-106, Glossary — verified yes
- RAL/REL equations (60 min of work per hour, 1-h TWA, WBGT in °C, M = metabolic rate in W) — "RAL [°C-WBGT] = 59.9 − 14.1 log10 M [W]" and "REL [°C-WBGT] = 56.7 − 11.5 log10 M [W]" — NIOSH 2016-106, Ch. 8.1, p. 93 — verified yes
- Values computed here from those equations (M in W: 117 / 233 / 349 / 465 / 580): RAL 30.7 / 26.5 / 24.0 / 22.3 / 20.9 °C; REL 32.9 / 29.5 / 27.5 / 26.0 / 24.9 °C. These are my arithmetic, not NIOSH's printed values. — computed here — verified yes (arithmetic), see 1.1 next two bullets for the printed values
- Work/rest versions of RAL and REL — "RALs and RELs take work/rest schedules into consideration so are shown for 60, 45, 30, and 15 minute time periods of work; see Figures 8-1 and 8-2." The 45/30/15 min curves exist only as graphs (Figures 8-1, 8-2); no equation or table is printed. — NIOSH 2016-106, Ch. 8.1 — verified yes (that they are graph-only). Numeric values for the 45/30/15 curves: NOT VERIFIED
- Printed worked examples (the only printed RAL/REL points) — "a vertical line at 300 kcal·h-1 (348.9 W) intersects the 60 min·h-1 REL curve at a WBGT of 27.8°C (82°F)" and "If the 70-kg worker was unacclimatized, Figure 8-1 indicates that metabolic heat measurement of the worker would be required above a WBGT of 25°C (77°F)." Note the graph readings (27.8, 25) differ by 0.3 and 1.0 °C from the equation values at 349 W (27.5, 24.0). — NIOSH 2016-106, Ch. 1.1.3 example box — verified yes
- REL by workload, acclimatized, as tabulated by NIOSH — Table 5-1, NIOSH column: Light "30°C (86°F) <200 kcal·h-1 (233 W)"; Moderate "28°C (82.4°F) 201–300 kcal·h-1 (234–349 W)"; Heavy "26°C (78.8°F) 301–400 kcal·h-1 (350–465 W)"; Very heavy "25°C (77°F) 401–500 kcal·h-1 (466–580 W)". — NIOSH 2016-106, Table 5-1, p. 70 — verified yes
- Same table, ACGIH column (this is the only publicly quotable ACGIH content I could open; it is NIOSH reproducing ACGIH, adapted from AIHA 2003): Light "30°C (86°F) 100–200 kcal·h-1"; Moderate "26.7°C (80°F) 201–350 kcal·h-1"; Very heavy "25°C (77°F) 350–500 kcal·h-1"; Heavy cell blank. — NIOSH 2016-106, Table 5-1 — verified yes (as reproduced). Current ACGIH TLV booklet (action limit / TLV by work-rest): NOT VERIFIED (paywalled, not opened)
- Clothing adjustments — "When a two-layer clothing ensemble is worn, the RAL and REL should be lowered by 2°C (3.8°F). When a partially air and/or vapor impermeable ensemble or heat reflective or protective aprons, leggings, gauntlets, etc., are worn, the RAL and REL should be lowered 4°C (7.2°F)." NIOSH adds these are "scientific judgments that have not been substantiated by controlled laboratory studies". — NIOSH 2016-106, Ch. 3.3 — verified yes
- Standard-man basis and applicability limit — "Values shown are for a "standard man" of 70 kg (154 lb) body weight and 1.8 m2 (19.4 ft2) body surface." and "the RALs and RELs may not be protective of everyone" — NIOSH 2016-106, Figs 8-1/8-2 captions and Ch. 8.1 — verified yes
- NIOSH removed the old ceiling limits — "Data and scientific evidence were not identified to continue to support the NIOSH ceiling limit recommendations." — NIOSH 2016-106, Ch. 8.1 — verified yes
- NIOSH work/rest example table (workers in normal clothing, "adjusted temperature" = air temperature adjusted for sun and humidity, not WBGT): Light work "Normal" until 105°F, "45/15" at 106°F, "30/30" at 109°F, "15/45" at 110°F; Heavy work "45/15" at 95°F, "30/30" at 100°F, "15/45" at 105°F. Adjustments: "Full sun (no clouds): Add 13°", "Partly cloudy/overcast: Add 7°", relative humidity "10%: Subtract 8°" … "60%: Add 9°". Footnote assumes workers "physically fit, well-rested, fully hydrated, under age 40" and "30% RH". — NIOSH 2016-106, Table 6-2, p. 76 — verified yes (partial extraction of the table; the 15-min-per-hour minimum rows only)

### 1.2 OSHA heat index bands and the OSHA-NIOSH Heat Safety Tool
- OSHA Heat Safety Tool hazard levels, current — "The App indicates the hazard levels using the heat index as: Caution less than 80°F HI, Warning 80°F to 94°F HI and Danger at 95°F HI or higher." — OSHA CPL 03-00-024 (10 Apr 2026), Sec. IV background (p. 5) and again in the enforcement procedures section — https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/enforcement/directives/CPL_03-00-024_0.pdf — verified yes
- Older OSHA four-level risk bands (Lower <91°F, Moderate 91–103°F, High 103–115°F, Very High/Extreme >115°F) — seen only in a web search summary and in archived OSHA pages (2012) that I could not open (the archived pages returned empty text). — NOT VERIFIED. Treat as superseded by the 80/95 bands above until the physician decides.
- National Weather Service heat index classes — "Caution 80°F - 90°F Fatigue possible with prolonged exposure and/or physical activity; Extreme Caution 90°F - 103°F Heat stroke, heat cramps, or heat exhaustion possible …; Danger 103°F - 124°F Heat cramps or heat exhaustion likely, and heat stroke possible …; Extreme Danger 125°F or higher Heat stroke highly likely" — NWS Amarillo, https://www.weather.gov/ama/heatindex — verified yes
- Heat index is calculated for shade and light wind — NWS: "the heat index values in the chart above are for shady locations. If you are exposed to direct sunlight, the heat index value can be increased by up to 15°F." OSHA's hazards page says direct sunlight "can increase Heat Index by up to 13.5°F (7.5°C)". — NWS Amarillo; OSHA https://www.osha.gov/heat-exposure/hazards — verified yes (the OSHA page text was read through a page summarizer, quote as returned; re-check wording on the live page before publishing)
- Heat index misses low-index fatalities — OSHA hazards page: "Outdoor workers have died of heat stroke when the day's maximum Heat Index was only 86°F." and "should not rely on Heat Index alone for the most accurate hazard assessment." — https://www.osha.gov/heat-exposure/hazards — verified yes (via summarizer, see above). OSHA NEP 2026 adds: "Heat-related fatalities have occurred with a heat index below 80°F, particularly when aggravating factors are present." — CPL 03-00-024 (2026) — verified yes

### 1.3 OSHA Heat National Emphasis Program (NEP) trigger
- Current directive and dates — "DIRECTIVE NUMBER: CPL 03-00-024 … National Emphasis Program – Outdoor and Indoor Heat-Related Hazards … SIGNATURE DATE: April 10, 2026 EFFECTIVE DATE: April 10, 2026". Cancels the 8 April 2022 version. "This Instruction shall be operative for no more than five years from the effective date" (so to April 2031 at the latest). — CPL 03-00-024 (2026), abstract and Sec. VII — verified yes
- Trigger — "A heat priority day occurs when the heat index for the day is expected to be 80°F or more." and "programmed inspections shall occur on any day that the NWS has announced a heat warning or advisory for the local area." — CPL 03-00-024 (2026), enforcement section — verified yes
- Rationale — "When the heat index is 80°F or higher, serious occupational heat-related illnesses and injuries become more frequent, especially in workplaces where unacclimatized workers are performing strenuous work" — CPL 03-00-024 (2026), Sec. IV — verified yes
- Scale — "approximately 2,400 heat-related hazard inspections each year, which included approximately 50 heat-related fatality inspections annually" (2022 to 2025, federal OSHA); "22 industries added, and 33 industries retained, for a total of 55 target industries." — CPL 03-00-024 (2026) — verified yes
- Link to severe injury reports — "This Heat NEP prioritizes on-site (in person) response for complaints and for all employer-reported hospitalizations (i.e., severe injury reports) related to heat hazards." — CPL 03-00-024 (2026) — verified yes
- Earlier extension (superseded) — CPL 03-01-024, 16 Jan 2025, extended the 2022 NEP "until April 8, 2026, or until superseded". — OSHA archive copy, https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/enforcement/directives/CPL-03-01-024.pdf — verified yes

### 1.4 OSHA proposed heat rule (NPRM, 30 Aug 2024)
Source for all of 1.4: OSHA, "Heat Injury and Illness Prevention in Outdoor and Indoor Work Settings", 89 Fed. Reg. 70698 (30 Aug 2024), Federal Register doc. 2024-14824, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/08/30/2024-14824 (full text downloaded and searched).
- Initial heat trigger — "Initial heat trigger means a heat index of 80 °F or a wet bulb globe temperature equal to the NIOSH Recommended Alert Limit (RAL)." — NPRM, proposed 29 CFR 1910.148 definitions and preamble — verified yes
- High heat trigger — "High heat trigger means a heat index of 90 °F or a wet bulb globe temperature equal to the NIOSH Recommended Exposure Limit (REL)." — NPRM — verified yes
- Controls at the high trigger — "a rest break of at least 15 minutes every two hours at or above the high heat trigger"; at the initial trigger the employer provides "a rest break if needed to prevent overheating". — NPRM, preamble comparison with ANSI/ASSP A10.50 — verified yes
- Why 80 °F — "researchers have identified a heat index of 80 °F as a highly sensitive trigger for heat-related fatalities (capturing 96-100% of fatalities) and nonfatalities (99-100%) among workers (excluding results from Washington State)." — NPRM, Sec. IV.B.C — verified yes (this is OSHA's summary; the underlying studies were not each opened)
- Why 90 °F — "Multiple studies additionally identified a rapidly declining sensitivity above a heat index of 90 °F" and Spector et al.: "75% of HRI claims occurred on days when the maximum heat index was at least 90 °F, whereas only 50% occurred on days when it was at least 99 °F and 25% for 106 °F." (45 agriculture and forestry claims, 1995–2009). — NPRM, Sec. IV.B — verified yes (OSHA's quotation of Spector)
- Washington ambient-temperature finding — "researchers in Washington found that 75-76% of HRI claims occurred on a day with a maximum ambient temperature of 80 °F or greater." — NPRM — verified yes
- WBGT triggers tested — "These exposure limits have been tested and found to be highly sensitive (100%) in modern laboratory conditions … Among workers in real-world settings, these WBGT-based exposure limits have been found to be highly sensitive for fatal outcomes (100% in one study; 92-100% in another) and, although slightly less so, still sensitive for nonfatal outcomes (73% in one study; 88-97% …" — NPRM — verified yes
- State comparators — Oregon and (proposed) Maryland use heat index "80 °F (Heat Index)" initial and "90 °F (Heat Index)" high; California outdoor "80 °F (Ambient)" and "95 °F (Ambient)"; Washington outdoor "80 °F (Ambient)" and "90 °F (Ambient)". — NPRM Table V-2 — verified yes
- ANSI/ASSP A10.50 comparator — "an action level of WBGT adjusted for clothing type (clothing-adjusted WBGT) of 70 °F or heat index adjusted for radiant heat (adjusted heat index) of 80 °F" and moderate hazard level "a clothing-adjusted WBGT of 80 °F or an adjusted heat index of 95 °F". — NPRM — verified yes
- Status of the rule — the NPRM is still a proposal. A web search (law-firm summaries, not the Federal Register or reginfo.gov) reports no final rule, further hearings in summer 2025, and a supplemental proposal targeted for Dec 2026. — NOT VERIFIED against an official source. Do not describe the rule as in force.

## 2. Estimating WBGT from weather data

### 2.1 Liljegren et al. 2008
- Model and accuracy — "The model is composed of separate submodels of the natural wet bulb and globe temperatures that are based on fundamental principles of heat and mass transfer, has no site-dependent parameters, and achieves an accuracy of better than 1 degree C based on comparisons with wet bulb globe temperature measurements at all depots." Inputs per the abstract: "standard meteorological data". — Liljegren JC, Carhart RA, Lawday P, Tschopp S, Sharp R. Modeling the wet bulb globe temperature using standard meteorological measurements. J Occup Environ Hyg 2008 Oct. PMID 18668404; DOI 10.1080/15459620802310770 — verified yes (abstract via NCBI E-utilities)
- Which inputs it needs (air temperature, humidity, wind speed, pressure, solar radiation, and sun position) and its stated limits (US Army depot sites, protective-clothing context) — not stated in the abstract; full paper not opened. — NOT VERIFIED
- Accuracy in Gulf conditions (hot, humid, dusty, high solar load) — no source found. — NOT VERIFIED (see Open questions)

### 2.2 Australian Bureau of Meteorology simplified WBGT
- Formula — "WBGT = 0.567 × Ta + 0.393 × e + 3.94 where: Ta = Dry bulb temperature (°C) e = Water vapour pressure (hPa) [humidity]" and "e = rh / 100 × 6.105 × exp ( 17.27 × Ta / ( 237.7 + Ta ) )" — BoM, Thermal Comfort observations, http://www.bom.gov.au/info/thermal_stress/ — verified yes. The page attributes the formula to the American College of Sports Medicine, "Prevention of thermal injuries during distance running - Position Stand. Med.J.Aust. 1984 Dec."
- Stated limits — "estimation of the WBGT under moderately sunny light wind conditions. Real variations of sunshine and wind are not taken into account. The formula is likely to overestimate the WBGT in cloudy or windy conditions, or when the sun is low or below the horizon. Under clear full sun and low humidity conditions the approximation underestimates the WBGT slightly." — BoM, same page — verified yes
- Computed here, as a warning: at 35 °C and 60% RH the formula gives 37.0 °C; at 45 °C and 50% RH it gives 48.2 °C. It has no solar or wind input, so on a shaded or windy day it will sit well above the NIOSH limits (all at or below 33 °C). It has not been checked against measured Gulf WBGT. — computed here — verified yes (arithmetic only)

### 2.3 Stull 2011 wet-bulb temperature
- Stated validity and error — "valid for relative humidities between 5% and 99% and for air temperatures between −20° and 50°C, except for situations having both low humidity and cold temperature. Over the valid range, errors in wet-bulb temperature range from −1° to +0.65°C, with mean absolute error of less than 0.3°C." Sea-level pressure only ("at standard sea level pressure"). — Stull R. Wet-bulb temperature from relative humidity and air temperature. J Appl Meteorol Climatol 2011 Nov. DOI 10.1175/JAMC-D-11-0143.1 — verified yes (abstract via Crossref API). The paper itself is not in PubMed and the AMS site blocked download.
- Equation (T in °C, RH in %): Tw = T·atan[0.151977·(RH + 8.313659)^0.5] + atan(T + RH) − atan(RH − 1.676331) + 0.00391838·RH^1.5·atan(0.023101·RH) − 4.686035. — coefficients match independent code in NCAR NCL (`heat_stress.ncl`), pythermalcomfort, CityEnergyAnalyst, and CTSM (found by GitHub code search); not read from the paper itself — verified partial. I checked it numerically against an exact psychrometric solve: 20 °C/50% RH gives 13.70 vs 13.88 °C; 40 °C/30% gives 25.92 vs 25.32 °C; 45 °C/50% gives 35.19 vs 34.66 °C (computed here, within the stated −1 to +0.65 °C).
- Note: Stull gives the psychrometric (aspirated) wet-bulb, not the natural wet-bulb that WBGT uses. It is an input to a WBGT estimate, not a WBGT estimate. — reasoning, flagged for the physician's reviewer — verified n/a

### 2.4 NWS heat index (Rothfusz regression)
- Regression — "HI = -42.379 + 2.04901523*T + 10.14333127*RH - .22475541*T*RH - .00683783*T*T - .05481717*RH*RH + .00122874*T*T*RH + .00085282*T*RH*RH - .00000199*T*T*RH*RH where T is temperature in degrees F and RH is relative humidity in percent." Adjustments: subtract "[(13-RH)/4]*SQRT{[17-ABS(T-95.)]/17}" if RH < 13% and T 80–112 °F; add "[(RH-85)/10] * [(87-T)/5]" if RH > 85% and T 80–87 °F. — NWS Weather Prediction Center, https://www.wpc.ncep.noaa.gov/html/heatindex_equation.shtml, citing Rothfusz, NWS Technical Attachment SR 90-23 (1990) — verified yes
- Limits — "The Rothfusz regression is not appropriate when conditions of temperature and humidity warrant a heat index value below about 80 degrees F." (a simpler Steadman-based formula is used there) and "is not valid for extreme temperature and relative humidity conditions beyond the range of data considered by Steadman." Regression error "±1.3 °F" (NWS Amarillo). — NWS WPC; NWS Amarillo — verified yes
- Computed here: 100 °F and 55% RH gives 123.6 °F (NWS Amarillo example: "will be 124 ° F") — matches. — verified yes
- The NWS heat index assumes shade, light wind, and a standard person, and Abu Dhabi regularly exceeds the validated range (above about 110 °F with low RH). Whether the OSHA trigger should be applied beyond the regression's range is an Open question. — reasoning — verified n/a

## 3. Acclimatisation and the first days on the job

- NIOSH schedule, new workers — "For new workers, the schedule should be no more than 20% of the usual duration of work in the hot environment on day 1 and a no more than 20% increase on each additional day." — NIOSH 2016-106, Table 4-1 (Acclimatization in workers) — verified yes
- NIOSH schedule, experienced workers — "For workers who have had previous experience with the job, the acclimatization regimen should be no more than 50% of the usual duration of work in the hot environment on day 1, 60% on day 2, 80% on day 3, and 100% on day 4." — NIOSH 2016-106, Table 4-1 — verified yes
- Duration and loss — "Gradually increase exposure time in hot environmental conditions over a period of 7 to 14 days." "Absence from work in the heat for a week or more results in a significant loss in the beneficial adaptations". "Can be regained in 2 to 3 days upon return to a hot job." "The time required for non–physically fit individuals to develop acclimatization is about 50% greater than for the physically fit." — NIOSH 2016-106, Table 4-1 — verified yes
- OSHA proposed rule uses the same schedule — Option B: "restricting employee exposure to heat to no more than 20% of a normal work shift exposure duration on the first day of work and increasing exposure by 20% of the work shift exposure duration on each subsequent day from day 2 through 4. This is consistent with NIOSH's recommended acclimatization plan" — NPRM, discussion of (e)(7)(i)(B) — verified yes
- Arbury 2014 (MMWR), 2012–2013 federal OSHA general-duty-clause heat cases — "20 cases of heat illness or death … In 13 cases, a worker died from heat exposure … Nine of the deaths occurred in the first 3 days of working on the job, four of them occurring on the worker's first day." "no provision was made for the acclimatization of new workers." Derived: 9/13 = 69% in first 3 days; 4/13 = 31% on day 1 (OSHA's NPRM states the same: "4 (31%) … 9 (69%)"). — Arbury S et al. Heat illness and death among workers, United States, 2012–2013. MMWR 2014;63(31):661-665. PMID 25102413; PMC4584656 — verified yes
- Arbury 2016 (J Occup Environ Med), all 84 OSHA heat enforcement cases of 2012–2013 — the abstract does not give first-days percentages (it says employers lacked "acclimatizing and training workers"). The NPRM reports: "Of the 23 cases involving a heat-related fatality, 17 (74%) occurred in the worker's first three days on the job and 8 (35%) on the worker's first day". — Arbury S, Lindsley M, Hodgson M. J Occup Environ Med 2016;58(4):359-363 (citation as given in the NPRM reference list). PMID 27058475; DOI 10.1097/JOM.0000000000000640 — verified partial (abstract yes; percentages secondary through the NPRM)
- Tustin 2018 (J Occup Environ Med case series), OSHA investigations 2011–2016 — "Most (73%) fatal HRIs occurred during the first week on the job." NPRM adds: "Among the fatality cases with job tenure information (n=22), 45.5% occurred on the first day of or returning to the job and 72.8% occurred during the first week. Among the non-fatal HRI cases … (n=32), 3.1% occurred on the first day and 18.7% occurred during the first week." — Tustin AW et al. Risk Factors for Heat-Related Illness in U.S. Workers: An OSHA Case Series. J Occup Environ Med 2018. PMID 29851740; DOI 10.1097/JOM.0000000000001365 — verified yes (73% from abstract; the 45.5/72.8/3.1/18.7 figures secondary through the NPRM)
- Tustin 2018 (MMWR), 25 outdoor cases, 2011–2016 — "Unacclimatized to heat, no. (%)": fatal 11 (78.6%) of 14; nonfatal 1 (9.1%) of 11; total 12 (48.0%). Workers were "considered unacclimatized if they had started a new job within the preceding 2 weeks or if they had recently r[eturned]…". — Tustin AW et al. Evaluation of Occupational Exposure Limits for Heat Stress in Outdoor Workers, United States, 2011–2016. MMWR 2018 Jul 6. PMID 29975679; DOI 10.15585/mmwr.mm6726a1; PMC6048976 — verified yes (Table 1)
- Same paper, limit performance — "Heat stress exceeded exposure limits in all 14 fatalities and in eight of 11 nonfatal illnesses." "The sensitivity of the NIOSH exposure limits was 100% (14 of 14) for detection of fatal heat stress and 72.7% (eight of 11) for detection of conditions that caused nonfatal illness." Range of effective WBGT "79°F–94°F (26.1°C–34.4°C)". — same — verified yes
- Same paper, Heat Index screen — "when WBGT is unavailable, a Heat Index screening threshold of 85°F (29.4°C) could identify potentially hazardous levels of workplace environmental heat." In the 21 cases in normal single-layer clothing "the minimum Heat Index was 85°F (29.4°C)". The authors' sample is 25 cases from OSHA investigations (selection bias; not a rate). — same — verified yes
- OSHA's own summary of the first-days problem — "the percent of workers who have been in their job for a month or less ranges from 3.7%-4.1% (BLS JOLTS 2023)" in summer months, against the shares above. — NPRM — verified yes (secondary use of JOLTS)

## 4. Ambient heat and occupational injury or illness incidence

- Park, Pankratz & Behrer — "Using data covering the universe of injury claims from the nation's largest worker's compensation system (2001-2018) … Hotter temperature increases workplace injuries significantly, causing approximately 20,000 injuries per year. The effects persist in both outdoor and indoor settings … We document a decline in the heat-sensitivity of injuries over the study period". — Park RJ, Pankratz N, Behrer AP. Temperature, Workplace Safety, and Labor Market Inequality. SSRN 3892588 (2021); DOI 10.2139/ssrn.3892588 — verified yes (abstract via Crossref). Published journal version not located; PubMed does not index it.
- Park et al. effect sizes as quoted by OSHA — "In comparison to a day with temperatures in the 60s °F, the risk of occupational heat-related injury increased by 5-7% (p<0.05) and 10-15% (p<0.05) on days with high temperatures between 85-90 °F and above 100 °F, respectively" and "over 11 million workers' compensation records in California". — NPRM — verified partial (secondary; I did not open the paper's tables, so confirm 5-7% and 10-15% against the paper before quoting)
- Spector 2016, outdoor agricultural workers, Washington State, 12,213 new traumatic injury claims 2000–2012 — "The traumatic injury odds ratio was 1.14 (95% confidence interval 1.06, 1.22), 1.15 (95% confidence interval 1.06, 1.25), and 1.10 (95% confidence interval 1.01, 1.20) for daily maximum Humidex of 25-29, 30-33, and ≥34, respectively, compared to < 25" — Spector JT et al. A Case-Crossover Study of Heat Exposure and Injury Risk in Outdoor Agricultural Workers. PLoS One 2016. PMID 27716794; DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0164498 — verified yes
- Calkins 2019, outdoor construction, Washington State, 63,720 claims 2000–2012 — "The traumatic injury odds ratio (OR) was 1.005 [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.003-1.007] per one °C change in humidex." Higher effects "among younger (18-24 years) and older (>54 years) workers … workers with less job experience". — Calkins MM et al. Scand J Work Environ Health 2019 Nov 1. PMID 30869152; DOI 10.5271/sjweh.3814 — verified yes
- Meta-analysis, Fatima 2021 — 22 studies, almost 22 million injuries, six countries: "the overall risk of OI increased by 1% (RR 1.010, 95% CI: 1.009-1.011) for 1 °C increase in temperature above reference values and 17.4% (RR 1.174, 95% CI: 1.057-1.291) during HW [heatwaves]." Humid subtropical: "RR 1.017, 95% CI: 1.014-1.020" per °C. "No studies assessing the risk of OI in Tropical regions were found." Effect has "a lag effect of 1-2 days". Higher risk in workers aged under 35, men, and agriculture/forestry/fishing, construction, manufacturing. — Fatima SH, Rothmore P, Giles LC, Varghese BM, Bi P. Extreme heat and occupational injuries in different climate zones: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Environ Int 2021 Mar. PMID 33472088; DOI 10.1016/j.envint.2021.106384 — verified yes. Note: "Hot Mediterranean … RR 1.009" is the nearest climate class to the UAE desert; there is no hot-desert class in the abstract.
- Heinzerling 2020, California workers' compensation, heat-related illness (not injury) — "15,996 HRI cases during 2000-2017 (6.0 cases/100,000 workers)"; highest rates "Agriculture, Farming, Fishing, and Forestry (38.6)", "Firefighters … (389.6)". — Heinzerling A et al. Risk factors for occupational heat-related illness among California workers, 2000-2017. Am J Ind Med 2020 Dec. PMID 33075156; DOI 10.1002/ajim.23191 — verified yes
- Undercount in official statistics — "An examination of workers' compensation claims in California … identified 3 to 6 times the number of annual heat-related illness and injury cases than reported by BLS SOII (Heinzerling et al., 2020)" and, comparing Park (about 20,000 heat-attributable injuries a year) with Heinzerling (an average of 889 HRI claims a year): "a 22-fold difference". — NPRM — verified partial (secondary; the arithmetic of 22-fold is OSHA's)
- Heat acts with a lag and across injury types — Fatima: "lag effect of 1-2 days". Park abstract: injuries "ostensibly unrelated to temperature (e.g. falling from heights)". — as above — verified yes
- Not found: a heat-injury study from the Gulf region. A PubMed search (Gulf / UAE / Qatar workers heat injury) was not run to completion in this pass. — NOT VERIFIED (gap)

## 5. UAE and Abu Dhabi rules

### 5.1 UAE federal midday break (2026)

## 6. OSHA Severe Injury Reports: coding, reporting rules, limits

### 6.1 Reporting rule, 29 CFR 1904.39
Source: eCFR, 29 CFR 1904.39 (as of 5 Nov 2026), https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1904/subpart-E/section-1904.39 — verified yes
- Requirement — "(1) Within eight (8) hours after the death of any employee as a result of a work-related incident, you must report the fatality … (2) Within twenty-four (24) hours after the in-patient hospitalization of one or more employees or an employee's amputation or an employee's loss of an eye, as a result of a work-related incident, you must report …"
- Time limit that matters for heat — "For an in-patient hospitalization, amputation, or loss of an eye, you must only report the event to OSHA if it occurs within twenty-four (24) hours of the work-related incident." A fatality is reportable only if it "occurs within thirty (30) days of the work-related incident". A heat case admitted on day 2 is not reportable.
- Definition — "in-patient hospitalization as a formal admission to the in-patient service of a hospital or clinic for care or treatment." "you do not have to report an in-patient hospitalization that involves only observation or diagnostic testing."
- If the employer learns late — report within "Eight (8) hours for a fatality, and twenty-four (24) hours for an in-patient hospitalization" after learning of it (and of its work-relatedness).
- Exclusions — a motor vehicle accident on a public street or highway outside a construction work zone; events on a commercial or public transportation system. Reports go by phone or in person to the nearest OSHA Area Office, to 1-800-321-OSHA, or through the online application on osha.gov.
- Federal coverage — "The reports will be updated periodically and represent incidents under federal OSHA jurisdiction only." and "They do not include incidents in states covered by OSHA-approved State Plans." "The requirement began on January 1, 2015." — OSHA Severe Injury Reports page, https://www.osha.gov/severeinjury (read through a page summarizer) — verified yes

## Proposed alert criteria for sign-off

All proposals are candidates for the physician to accept, change or reject. None is clinically validated for the UAE. Each cites the bullet it comes from.

A. Environmental alert tiers (screen with the weather-derived index; confirm with WBGT where available)
1. Tier 1, "heat priority": heat index >= 80 °F (26.7 °C), or modelled WBGT at or above the NIOSH RAL for the assumed workload. Source: section 1.3 (OSHA NEP trigger), 1.4 (OSHA initial heat trigger), 1.1 (RAL equation). Caveat: heat index is shade-based, and Tier 1 will be on for most of an Abu Dhabi summer.
2. Tier 2, "high heat": heat index >= 90 °F (32.2 °C), or modelled WBGT at or above the NIOSH REL for the workload (REL light 30 °C, moderate 28 °C, heavy 26 °C, very heavy 25 °C). Source: section 1.4 (OSHA high heat trigger), 1.1 (Table 5-1). Lower by 2 °C for two-layer clothing and 4 °C for impermeable clothing (1.1).
3. Tier 3, "danger", optional: heat index >= 95 °F (35 °C), matching the Heat Safety Tool "Danger" band; or the Abu Dhabi TWL below 115 W/m² when TWL inputs exist. Source: 1.2 (HST bands, NEP 2026), 5.2 (TWL high-risk zone).
4. Screening alternative for the backtest only: heat index >= 85 °F (29.4 °C) for workers in normal clothing. Source: section 3 (Tustin MMWR 2018, "minimum Heat Index was 85°F"). Small sample (21 cases); do not use as a standard.
5. Abu Dhabi programme trigger: any forecast shift temperature >= 35 °C starts the employer heat stress programme unless a TWL assessment shows no risk. Source: 5.2 (CoP 11.0 Sec. 3.1.1(e)).

B. Worker-status modifiers (cannot be inferred from weather; need a roster field or a default)
6. Unacclimatised if started a new job within the preceding 2 weeks, or returned after more than 14 days away (Abu Dhabi TWL notes) or "a week or more" (NIOSH). Unacclimatised workers use the RAL, not the REL, and the TWL zones forbid them from medium and high risk work. Source: 3 (Tustin MMWR), 1.1, 5.2.
7. First-week risk uplift: raise the alert one tier for workers in days 1 to 5 (new workers), and use the 20 / 40 / 60 / 80 / 100% exposure schedule (Abu Dhabi TG) or the NIOSH 20% rule. Source: section 3 (Arbury 2014: 9 of 13 deaths in first 3 days; Tustin 2018: 73% of fatal HRIs in the first week), 5.2.
8. Early-season/heat-wave rule: treat the first hot days of the season, and any day after a cool spell, as an acclimatisation-loss event for the whole crew. Source: 3 (Tustin MMWR recommends "an acclimatization schedule for newly hired workers and unacclimatized long-term workers (e.g., during early-season heat waves)"). Threshold for "cool spell" is not sourced.

C. Actions attached to tiers (what the alert says)
9. Tier 1: water, shade, rest "if needed", watch new workers. Source: 1.4 (NPRM initial trigger controls), 5.2.
10. Tier 2: scheduled rest, at least 15 minutes every two hours (OSHA), or NIOSH/Abu Dhabi work-rest (heavy work 20 work / 40 rest in the TWL high zone; 45/15 in the medium zone for heavy work). Source: 1.4, 5.2.
11. UAE midday ban 12:30–15:00, 15 Jun–15 Sep, as a fixed schedule overlay. Source: 5.1.

D. Back-test design (US data)
12. Case definition: OSHA SIR rows with EventTitle containing "environmental heat" (531x), plus Nature in the heat family. Source: 6.2.
13. Evaluate: share of cases on days with heat index (or modelled WBGT) above each tier threshold at the incident location (sensitivity), and the share of all summer person-days flagged (alert burden). Do not report a false-alarm rate or PPV: SIR has only cases, no denominators. Source: 1.4 (OSHA's own sensitivity framing), 6.3.
14. State the data limits on every result: federal-OSHA states only; 24-hour hospitalisation rule; probable heat under-reporting (Illinois: 25.1% for illnesses); no tenure field. Source: 6.1, 6.3.

## Open questions

1. Which workload class and clothing class apply to the default worker (outdoor construction in Abu Dhabi: moderate or heavy, one layer or coverall)? The RAL/REL choice depends on it, and WBGT estimates have no workload input.
2. Metabolic-rate-dependent RAL/REL values for 45/30/15 min of work per hour exist only as graphs in NIOSH 2016. Digitise Figures 8-1 and 8-2 or restrict the pack to the 60-min equations?
3. Does the physician accept the heat index (shade basis, assumed validity to about 110 °F at low humidity) as the screen in the Gulf, or require a WBGT or TWL model? TWL needs globe temperature and wind; the TG gives no equation and the online calculator link is blank. The Brake and Bates TWL algorithm was not opened.
4. Accuracy of weather-derived WBGT in Abu Dhabi: no source found. Liljegren's "better than 1 degree C" was shown at US Army depots. Is there a validation against Gulf field WBGT?
5. Park et al. 5–7% and 10–15% effect sizes and Arbury 2016 first-three-day percentages are secondary (via the OSHA NPRM). Open the primary papers before they appear in any slide or text.

7. Official mohre.gov.ae page for the 2026 midday break and the legal text (Ministerial Resolution 44 of 2022 per MoHRE's sustainability page) were not opened; the fine is from a newspaper.

12. The two schedules (NIOSH 7–14 days, CoP "5-7 days", TG 5 days) differ. Which does the product adopt as the default for the 'unacclimatised' flag?
