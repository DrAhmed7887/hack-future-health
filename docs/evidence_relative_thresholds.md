# Evidence pack: heat-health thresholds are location-relative

Claim under test: "Heat-health thresholds are location-specific; heat warning systems and epidemiology set them relative to local climate (e.g. local percentiles), because populations adapt to their usual climate."

Method: each source below was opened on 7 Nov 2026 (PubMed abstracts via NCBI E-utilities efetch; NWS and WHO/WMO pages and PDF downloaded and read as text). Quotes are verbatim from those texts. Page/line pointers for the WHO/WMO PDF refer to its text extraction. Nothing here was cited from memory.

Scope note: abstracts only for papers (full text not read). "Verified yes" = the quoted text was seen in the source opened.

## 1. Multi-country temperature-mortality: minimum-mortality temperature is a local percentile

- Minimum-mortality temperature differs by climate. Quote: "The temperature percentile of minimum mortality varied from roughly the 60th percentile in tropical areas to about the 80-90th percentile in temperate regions." Source: Gasparrini A et al., Lancet 2015;386:369-75, "Mortality risk attributable to high and low ambient temperature: a multicountry observational study". PMID 26003380, DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(14)62114-0, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26003380/. Verified: yes (abstract).
- Study design (384 locations, 13 countries; optimum = point of minimum mortality): "We collected data for 384 locations in Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, Italy, Japan, South Korea, Spain, Sweden, Taiwan, Thailand, UK, and USA." and "heat and cold, defined as temperatures above and below the optimum temperature, which corresponded to the point of minimum mortality". Same source. Verified: yes.
- Caution for the write-up: the abstract gives the percentile range of minimum mortality (about 60th to 80-90th), not "the 75th percentile" or any single number. Do not state a single figure from this paper.

## 2. Percentile-defined heat waves and acclimatisation evidence

- Heat wave defined locally. Quote: "Heat waves were defined as ≥ 2 days with temperature ≥ 95th percentile for the community for 1 May through 30 September." Source: Anderson GB, Bell ML, Environ Health Perspect 2011;119:210-8, "Heat waves in the United States ... 43 U.S. communities". PMID 21084239, DOI 10.1289/ehp.1002313. Verified: yes.
- Early-season heat is worse. Quote: "Mortality increased 5.04% (95% PI, 3.06-7.06%) during the first heat wave of the summer versus 2.65% (95% PI, 1.14-4.18%) during later heat waves, compared with non-heat wave days." Same source. Verified: yes.
- Regional difference. Quote: "Heat wave mortality impacts and effect modification by heat wave characteristics were more pronounced in the Northeast and Midwest compared with the South." Same source. Verified: yes.
- Direct evidence of acclimatisation (strongest for the "because" clause). Quote: "Larger spatial heterogeneity for absolute temperature estimates (comparing risk at specific temperatures) than for relative temperature estimates (comparing risk at community-specific temperature percentiles) provides evidence for acclimatization." Also: "Spatial heterogeneity in effects indicates that weather-mortality relationships from 1 community may not be applicable in another." Also the exposure metric: "comparing the 99th and 90th percentile temperatures for the community". Source: Anderson BG, Bell ML, Epidemiology 2009;20:205-13, "Weather-related mortality: how heat, cold, and heat waves affect mortality in the United States" (107 US communities). PMID 19194300, DOI 10.1097/EDE.0b013e318190ee08. Verified: yes (abstract). Note: this is the 2009 paper in Epidemiology, not EHP.

## 3. Operational systems

### US National Weather Service HeatRisk
- Source opened: https://www.wpc.ncep.noaa.gov/heatrisk/overview.html (NWS Weather Prediction Center, HeatRisk Overview; page labels the product "experimental"). Verified: yes.
- Location- and date-specific thresholds, acclimation. Quote: "the HeatRisk identifies unusual heat (defined as the warmest 5% of temperatures) specifically for a particular date and location, resulting in daily thresholds that are unique for each location and date. This allows the approach to better account for acclimation (many of us become 'used to the heat' and so our thresholds change) and the variation in climatology that we know exists across most regions of the United States." Verified: yes.
- Climatology plus health data. Quote: "HeatRisk uses a high-resolution gridded climatology to put the forecast into context and also incorporates heat-health data from the CDC to influence the local thresholds and inform the approach." Verified: yes.
- Compared with local history, day and night. Quote: "HeatRisk utilizes both the high and low temperatures for a location and compares them to historical values at that location to classify those temperatures that are in the top 5% and above levels identified by the CDC heat-health data as excessive for that climate." Verified: yes.
- At-risk group wording. Quote: "Those not acclimated to the level of heat expected - especially those who are new to a much warmer climate". Verified: yes.
- Caution: HeatRisk is labelled experimental and is not the official NWS heat warning (a search summary said so; I did not locate that sentence on the page I opened, so treat "not the official warning product" as NOT VERIFIED here). The detail that the yellow and red levels map to the CDC minimum-mortality temperature and the 50th percentile of heat-attributable deaths came only from a search-engine summary: NOT VERIFIED (the HeatRisk v2.5 overview PDF, https://www.wpc.ncep.noaa.gov/heatrisk/pdf/HeatRisk-v2.5-Overview.pdf, was not opened).

### WHO/WMO Heatwaves and Health: Guidance on Warning-System Development (WMO-No. 1142, 2015)
- Source opened: PDF at https://www.uncclearn.org/wp-content/uploads/library/who16102015.pdf (ISBN 978-92-63-11142-5). Verified: yes. Section 4.3-4.4 "How warning thresholds are determined".
- Thresholds are climate-specific. Quote: "Single-day thresholds are climate-specific, ranging from 30°C in Belarus to 38°C in Greece (WHO, 2009)." Verified: yes.
- Min/max thresholds vary by region. Quote: "Thresholds vary from 15°C in parts of England to 25°C in parts of Spain for minimum temperatures, with maxima from 28°C in England to 41°C in Spain (WHO, 2009)." Verified: yes.
- Percentile thresholds. Quote: "In a number of HHWSs, the threshold is set to a percentile. In Belgium, for example, the 95th percentile of summer maximum temperature is the threshold (SPF, 2005)." Verified: yes.
- France adjusts for climatological differences. Quote: "In a number of systems, such as in France (Figure 5), the threshold is defined to account for climatological differences (InVS, 2005)". Verified: yes.
- Acclimatisation and similar temperatures. Quote: "Similar temperatures can have different impacts, depending on the duration of the event or the acclimatization status of the population, which is related to the time in the season when the heatwave occurred, as well as the long-term climate." Verified: yes.
- Honest counterpoint for the write-up: the same section says "In many other cases, however, the thresholds have been somewhat arbitrarily set," and that percentile and fixed thresholds such as the US heat index 41°C "have not been developed from, nor are they related to, any specific health response." So "set relative to local climate" is common but not universal, and not all thresholds are health-derived. I found no sentence in the guidance that explicitly says thresholds "should" be locally derived (the text I searched for that exact wording was not found); the support is descriptive. Phoenix example in the same text uses a local, season-smoothed threshold up to 45°C in July.

## 4. Occupational evidence

- Heat-occupational-injury risk depends on climate zone. Quote: "The risk of OI associated with extreme heat is not evenly distributed and is dependent on underlying climatic conditions, workers' attributes, the nature of work and workplace characteristics." Also: "Among different climate zones, the highest risk of OI during hot temperatures was identified in Humid Subtropical Climates (RR 1.017, 95% CI: 1.014-1.020) followed by Oceanic (RR 1.010, 95% CI: 1.008-1.012) and Hot Mediterranean Climates (RR 1.009, 95% CI: 1.008-1.011)." And: "No studies assessing the risk of OI in Tropical regions were found." Source: Varghese BM et al. (authorship not shown in the abstract text I fetched; confirm before naming), Environ Int 2021;148:106384, "Extreme heat and occupational injuries in different climate zones: A systematic review and meta-analysis". PMID 33472088, DOI 10.1016/j.envint.2021.106384. Verified: yes for text; author attribution NOT VERIFIED from the fetched abstract. Note the review found no desert or Gulf-climate studies.
- Acclimatisation and change relative to recent days (Washington State). Quote: "Insufficient heat acclimatization is a risk factor for heat-related illness (HRI) morbidity, particularly during periods of sudden temperature increase." and "Claims occurring on 'cluster' days ... had both a significantly higher mean DOI Tmax,PRISM (99.3°F vs. 85.8°F [37.4°C vs. 29.9°C]) and a higher proportion of 'sudden increase' claims (80.2% vs. 24.3%)." and "Seventy-six percent of analyzed HRI claims occurred on days with a Tmax,PRISM ≥ 80°F." Source: Washington State workers' compensation HRI claims 2006-2021 (1,241 claims), Am J Ind Med 2023;66:623-636. PMID 37291066, DOI 10.1002/ajim.23506. Verified: yes. Author names not shown in the fetched text (the request suggested Spector/Bonauto; not confirmed from the abstract).
- Burden varies by climate zone (Australia). Quote: "By climate zone, the heat-attributable proportion was largest in the tropical Am (12 DALYs; 3.5%) and Aw zones (34 DALYs; 3.5%)". Source: Med J Aust 2023;219:542-548, "The burden of occupational injury attributable to high temperatures in Australia, 2014-19". PMID 37992722, DOI 10.5694/mja2.52171. Verified: yes. Authors not shown (Varghese is plausible, NOT VERIFIED).
- Italy, not an explicit threshold study. Quote: "Compared with the 75th percentile (22.4 °C UTCI), the 99th percentile (31.2 °C UTCI) was associated with an odds ratio of 1.61 [1.22-2.14]". Source: Environ Res 2026;302:124664, case-crossover of fatal occupational injuries, Italy. PMID 42097385, DOI 10.1016/j.envres.2026.124664. Verified: yes (abstract). Uses percentiles of the national UTCI distribution; do not present it as showing that thresholds differ by region. A Morabito-authored Italian threshold study was not located: NOT VERIFIED.

## 5. UAE / Gulf heat and workers

- UAE industrial sites, conditions. Quote: "...extreme heat stress conditions experienced by workers in the United Arab Emirates and other Middle Eastern countries each summer, where ambient air temperatures often reach 45°C (135°F) and higher with 90% humidity." Also: "465 companies employing 814 996 heat-exposed workers across 6254 work and labor residence sites were reached" and "one of which reported a combined 79.5% decrease in cases (15.3 vs 1.16 cases per 1000 workers)". Source: Joubert D, Thomsen J, Harrison O, Am J Public Health 2011;101:395-8, "Safety in the Heat" (Abu Dhabi). PMID 21307373, DOI 10.2105/AJPH.2009.189563. Verified: yes. Programme evaluation at two companies, not a population incidence estimate.
- UAE construction workers, thermal exposure. Quote: "the WBGT exceeded the recommended Threshold Limit Value (TLV) and that workers are at risk of heat stress" and "As per TWL, the site was labeled as Acclimatization Zone implying that no un-acclimatized worker should work here". Source: Ahmed HO et al., Ind Health 2020;58:170-181, University of Sharjah. PMID 31308288, DOI 10.2486/indhealth.2018-0259. Verified: yes. Single-site assessment.
- UAE self-pacing. Quote: "There was no evidence of an effect of variation in environmental thermal stress on either average working heart rate or aural temperature." (150 subjects, industrial sites in the UAE). Source: Miller V et al., Ann Occup Hyg 2011;55:548-55. PMID 21474543, DOI 10.1093/annhyg/mer012. Verified: yes. Relevant as a caution that workers adjust behaviour; not a threshold study.
- Qatar migrant workers (mortality). Quote: "The average annual death rate for NMWs in Qatar was 150 deaths/100,000." and "in this NMW population, the figures were 22% during the cool season and 58% during the hot season." (CVD share of deaths; "As many as 200 of the 571 CVD deaths during 2009-2017 could have been prevented"). Source: Heat Stress Impacts on Cardiac Mortality in Nepali Migrant Workers in Qatar, Cardiology 2019;143:37-48. PMID 31302648, DOI 10.1159/000500853. Verified: yes. Authors not shown; the authors themselves say cause-of-death records were poor, and attribution to heat is "likely", not proven.
- Gulf review. Quote: "Strengthening standardized clinical protocols and region-specific prevention policies is critical in the context of ongoing climate change." and "heat-related illnesses disproportionately affect outdoor workers, pilgrims, and vulnerable populations". Source: Naji B, Fredericks S, Cureus 2026;18(7):e113267, "Heat-Related Emergencies in the Gulf Region: A Literature Review". PMID 42634775, DOI 10.7759/cureus.113267. Verified: yes (abstract). Low-tier journal, narrative review; use only as a pointer.
- Migrant vs native workers (Greece, with Qatar-based co-author). Quote: "migrant workers from LMICs faced a 52% and 80% higher risk for experiencing core body temperature above the safety threshold of 38 °C". Source: Ioannou LG et al., J Immigr Minor Health 2023;25:816-823. PMID 37208495. Verified: yes. Greek agriculture, not Gulf.

## Suggested wording for the write-up (stays inside what was verified)

"Heat-health thresholds are location-specific. In a 384-location, 13-country study the temperature of minimum mortality sat at roughly the 60th percentile of local temperatures in tropical areas and about the 80-90th percentile in temperate regions (Gasparrini 2015, PMID 26003380). Across 107 US communities, risk was more uniform when compared at community-specific percentiles than at absolute temperatures, which the authors read as evidence of acclimatization (Anderson and Bell 2009, PMID 19194300). Operational systems follow this logic: the US NWS HeatRisk sets daily thresholds 'unique for each location and date', and the WHO/WMO guidance describes climate-specific and percentile-based thresholds across national systems, while noting some are set arbitrarily."
